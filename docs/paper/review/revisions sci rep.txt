Reviewer 1
This manuscript presents a well-conducted comparison between a radiomics-based machine-learning pipeline and a transfer-learning deep-learning model for predicting long-term treatment response from longitudinal T2-weighted MRI in a preclinical GL261 glioblastoma model. The topic is relevant and the analysis of temporal predictive behaviour and interpretability is a genuine strength. However, several methodological issues currently limit confidence in the reported performance and, particularly, in the claim of reliable early prediction.
The main concern is the very limited number of independent subjects. Although 169 MRI examinations are analysed, the core cured-vs-relapsing task is based on only 10 independent animals (5 + 5), with the reported metrics derived from multiple longitudinal images per animal. The Grouped Leave-One-Out (GLOO) scheme appropriately prevents image-level leakage and should be retained, but the effective number of independent observations remains that of ~10 subjects, and the reported metrics should be interpreted in that light rather than as if supported by 169 independent samples.
As a direct consequence, performance values reported to four decimal places (e.g., AUC 0.8511 vs. 0.7015) convey a level of precision that 10 subjects cannot support. All metrics in Table 1 should be accompanied by subject-level bootstrap confidence intervals, and the central comparison between the two pipelines should be supported by a formal test of the difference between the ROC curves (e.g., DeLong or a subject-level permutation test), so that it is possible to judge whether the observed gap is robust or within the range expected from reassigning a single animal across folds.
A related concern is the risk of information leakage during model development. The supplementary material appropriately clarifies that oversampling was restricted to the training partition of each fold. However, it remains unclear whether all radiomic feature selection, normalization, temporal feature engineering and hyperparameter optimization procedures were entirely nested within the training data of each outer GLOO fold. This is particularly important because 1,218 radiomic features were initially considered, with Mann–Whitney-based selection at an uncorrected p<0.05 threshold, followed by the generation of additional temporal descriptors. With only 10 independent animals, any step performed outside the fold would carry a substantial risk of overfitting and unstable feature selection, and would inflate the reported radiomics performance; please state unambiguously, for each step, whether it was fully nested within the training fold.
The same concern applies symmetrically to the deep-learning pipeline and should be described more explicitly at the subject level. Under GLOO, with a single animal held out for testing, it is not clear which data were used as the validation set for early stopping and for selecting the best checkpoint. If model selection relied in any way on the held-out subject, the reported AUC would be optimistically biased; confirmation that the validation partition never included the test subject is needed.
The internal consistency of the model comparison should also be clarified. In Table 1 the frozen feature-extractor configuration (EfficientNet FE + XGB) attains a higher AUC (0.8583) than the fine-tuned model presented as the best overall (0.8511), yet the abstract and conclusions foreground the fine-tuned model and an AUC of "0.85". The rationale — a more favourable sensitivity/specificity balance — is reasonable, but it should be stated explicitly which configuration is the final proposed model and on what basis it is selected.
Finally, some conclusions should be moderated. The longitudinal differences in selected radiomic features are illustrated descriptively as group-level trajectories, without individual trajectories, uncertainty estimates, or repeated-measures statistical testing, and should therefore be described as illustrative rather than as established biomarkers; the interpretation of increasing Dependence Entropy as a distinctive signature of effective therapy is likewise speculative on five cured animals and should be framed cautiously. Given the single experimental model, the small number of animals, and the absence of independent validation, the study should primarily be presented as a proof-of-concept rather than as evidence approaching clinical applicability, and the early-prediction claim in particular — resting on the smallest and most imbalanced portion of the cohort — warrants correspondingly cautious wording.
Reviewer 2
I recommend major revision rather than rejection at this stage.

My main concern is whether the reported CV performance is fully leakage-free. The manuscript does not clearly state whether outcome dependent radiomic feature selection, preprocessing, and hyperparameter optimization procedure were also restricted to subject-separated training data within each fold.

This distinction is important because the primary treatment-response analysis contains only 10 independent animals despite a much larger number of repeated MRIs. If feature or model selection was performed using the complete dataset, I would consider the reported performance estimates unreliable and believe reanalysis is necessary. If these procedures were already fully nested by animal, the manuscript appears addressable by major revision through clearer reporting, subject-level performance summaries, and revision of several conclusions. No additional animal experiments is needed
